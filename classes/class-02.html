4. Advantages and limitations

Seq-based Imaging based
Advantages Whole transcriptome, discovery. Detects unexpected genes. Workflow close to scRNA-seq. Low cost per gene. Visium has the largest user base and most validated tools True subcellular resolution. High sensitivity. Real per cell data (niches and contacts without deconvolution). Keeps morphology, integrates with H&E and IF. Works well with FFPE and degraded RNA
Limitations Spots mix cells, rare types get diluted. Needs deconvolution with a scRNA-seq reference. Low capture efficiency, sparse matrices. Poly-A only. Lateral diffusion blurs position. Permeabilization must be tuned per tissue. Small fixed area. High cost per sample. In Visium HD and Stereo-seq the bin is not a cell Closed panel, no discovery outside it. Segmentation is the main bottleneck: errors create chimeric profiles and false cell types. Optical crowding of abundant genes. Autofluorescence (lipofuscin, collagen, red blood cells). Huge data (hundreds of GB to TB). Long instrument time, high cost. Less mature analysis ecosystem

7. Single-cell vs. spatial: preprocessing and cell annotation

Both fields share the same conceptual pipeline — QC, normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, annotation. The difference is not the list of steps, it is what a row of the count matrix actually represents.

In scRNA-seq the cell is defined by the chemistry itself: the tissue is dissociated, one cell goes into one droplet, and every transcript in that droplet is by construction assigned to that cell. In spatial data the cell is never given. It has to be reconstructed computationally, and the way it is reconstructed depends entirely on the technology family.

7.1 What the measurement unit is

Technology family Unit in the matrix Relation to a cell
scRNA-seq / snRNA-seq Droplet or well One cell (barring doublets and empty droplets)
Spot-based (Visium, original ST, Slide-seq) Spot or bead Fixed capture area. A 55 µm Visium spot typically pools ~1 to 10 cells of mixed types. The cell does not exist in the data
High-resolution seq-based (Visium HD, Stereo-seq, Seq-Scope) Bin (arbitrary square, e.g. 2, 8 or 16 µm) A bin is a grid square, not a biological object. It may cut one cell across several bins, or pool fragments of neighbors
Imaging-based (Xenium, CosMx, MERSCOPE) Segmented cell Raw data is a point cloud of individual transcripts with (x, y) coordinates. A “cell” only exists after segmentation draws a boundary around a group of those points
Region-based (GeoMx, LCM) User-drawn ROI Whatever the user selected. No single-cell claim at all

The consequence: in spot- and bin-based data the analytical problem is mixture (which cell types compose this unit, and in what proportion). In imaging-based data the problem is assignment (which cell does this transcript belong to). These are different problems and they need different tools.

7.2 Preprocessing, step by step

Step scRNA-seq Spatial
QC metrics Counts per cell, genes per cell, % mitochondrial, doublet score Same metrics, but the thresholds mean something different. A low-count Visium spot may be a genuinely sparse tissue region, not a dying cell. In imaging-based data, counts per cell scale with the panel size (a 300-gene panel gives far fewer counts than a whole transcriptome) and with cell area, so a “low-count” cell may simply be a small lymphocyte
% mitochondrial Standard proxy for dying cells Often unusable: mitochondrial genes are frequently absent from targeted panels, and in FFPE the metric is not interpretable the same way. Needs different QC proxies
Doublet detection Core step (Scrublet, DoubletFinder) Meaningless in spot/bin data — every spot is a “multiplet” by design; the problem is handled by deconvolution instead. In imaging-based data, “doublets” are not chemistry artifacts but segmentation errors (two cells merged into one), which is a different failure mode and needs different diagnostics
Ambient RNA Free-floating RNA in the droplet suspension (SoupX, CellBender) The analogous problem is transcript spillover / lateral diffusion: RNA that physically drifted away from its cell of origin, or extracellular transcripts sitting between cells. Imaging platforms include negative control probes (blank codewords) to estimate the false-detection rate directly
Segmentation Not applicable — cells are already physically separated The central preprocessing step for imaging-based data. See 7.3
Deconvolution Not applicable Required for spot-based data to estimate cell-type proportions per spot (RCTD, Cell2location, SPOTlight, Stereoscope, cell2location)
Normalization Library size + log, or SCTransform Same formulas, but total counts per unit confound with cell/spot area and local tissue density, not only with capture efficiency. A dense lymphoid region will have higher counts per spot than sparse stroma for purely anatomical reasons — this is biology-driven, not technical, and normalizing it away can erase real signal
Batch / slide effects Between samples and chemistries Also between slides, capture areas and fields of view (FOVs) within the same experiment. FOV-edge artifacts are a known imaging-platform issue
Spatial-specific QC None Detecting which spots are actually under tissue, alignment/registration to the H&E or IF image, checking panel coverage for the cell types of interest, and inspecting the image itself for folds, tears and detachment

7.3 Why segmentation is the problem in imaging-based data

The raw data contains no cells. A Xenium or CosMx run outputs a list of decoded transcripts, each with a gene identity and an (x, y) — often (x, y, z) — coordinate. Nothing in that file says which transcript came from which cell. Building the count matrix means drawing boundaries on the image and summing the transcripts that fall inside each boundary. Everything downstream is therefore conditional on the quality of the cell-boundary detection. If the boundary is wrong, the expression profile is wrong, and no amount of clustering, normalization or annotation will recover it.

How the errors manifest

Error Mechanism Downstream consequence
False merge Two adjacent cells enclosed in one boundary A chimeric profile: a T cell fused with an epithelial cell produces a unit expressing both CD3E and EPCAM. It clusters separately and is easily mistaken for a novel “intermediate” or “transitional” cell state
False split One cell divided into two units Halves the counts, dilutes markers, creates artificially low-quality cells that get discarded by QC — biasing against large or irregularly shaped cell types
Transcript spillover Transcripts of cell A assigned to cell B The most insidious error: it does not look broken. Highly expressed genes from an abundant neighbor bleed into every surrounding cell, generating false co-expression and inflating apparent cell-cell “interaction” signal
Orphan transcripts Real transcripts with no assignable cell (e.g. the nucleus lies outside the section plane) Lost signal, systematically biased toward cell types with large or elongated morphology

The critical point is that these errors are indistinguishable from biology after the fact. A chimeric merged cell looks exactly like a real hybrid cell state. Spillover looks exactly like a real ligand-receptor neighborhood. This is why segmentation must be inspected on the image, not merely accepted as a preprocessing default.

Why nucleus expansion and Voronoi tessellation are weak solutions

Both are geometric shortcuts used because the nuclear stain (DAPI) is trivially easy to segment while the cell boundary is not. Both replace a biological boundary with a mathematical one.

Fixed-radius nucleus expansion — segment nuclei, then dilate each by a constant distance (commonly 5–15 µm) and call that the cell:

  • It assumes every cell has the same size and shape. A resting lymphocyte (~7 µm) and a macrophage, tumor cell or neuron (30 µm and up, with processes) get the same treatment. Small cells are over-expanded into their neighbors; large cells are truncated, losing most of their cytoplasmic transcripts.
  • Most mRNA is cytoplasmic, and cytoplasmic geometry is exactly what the nuclear stain does not tell you. Polarized epithelium, elongated fibroblasts, dendritic processes and neuronal projections are all badly approximated by a disc.
  • It fails hardest precisely where the biology is most interesting: densely packed tissue (lymph node, germinal centre, tumor nest), where every expansion necessarily crosses into a neighbor.

Voronoi / Delaunay tessellation — partition the plane so each point belongs to the nearest nucleus:

  • It partitions all available space, so every transcript is assigned to some cell. There is no “unassigned”, no extracellular compartment, no acellular matrix. Ambient and extracellular transcripts are forced into cells by construction.
  • It produces convex polygons that tile the plane, which no real tissue does. Non-convex, interdigitating and wrapping morphologies (podocytes, astrocytes, dendritic cells) cannot be represented.
  • Cells whose nucleus is outside the plane of section have no seed at all, so their territory is silently absorbed by neighbors — a 2D slice through a 3D tissue guarantees this happens.

The shared failure of both: they use only nuclear position, ignoring the transcript distribution itself and any real membrane information.

What actually improves segmentation

Strategy What it adds
Membrane and boundary protein staining The direct fix: image the actual cell border. Xenium’s multi-tissue stain (e.g. ATP1A1, E-cadherin, CD45) and CosMx’s morphology markers (PanCK, CD45, CD298/B2M, membrane markers) provide channels that mark membranes and compartments, so the boundary is measured rather than assumed. Choosing markers that cover your cell types matters — a panel with only epithelial and immune boundary markers will still segment stroma poorly
Deep-learning segmentation on those channels Cellpose, StarDist, Mesmer/DeepCell learn irregular, non-convex shapes from image data instead of imposing a disc or polygon. Only as good as the input stains, and usually benefits from tissue-specific fine-tuning
Transcript-aware / probabilistic segmentation Baysor, ClusterMap, JSTA, BIDCell, proseg use the transcript point cloud itself as evidence: transcripts with coherent expression identity are grouped together, boundaries follow molecular composition rather than geometry alone, and — crucially — transcripts can be left unassigned instead of being forced into a cell. Combining these with a membrane-stain prior generally outperforms either alone
3D / multi-plane imaging Reduces the orphan-nucleus problem caused by sectioning 3D cells into a 2D plane
Post-hoc segmentation QC Check for biologically impossible co-expression (CD3E with EPCAM, PTPRC with KRT), inspect the fraction of unassigned transcripts, compare counts-per-cell against cell area, and re-run with a second segmentation method to see which conclusions survive. If a “novel cell state” disappears under a different segmentation, it was an artifact
Segmentation-free analysis For some questions (spatial domains, gene co-localization, tissue niches) the cell is not required at all — analysing the transcript point cloud or binned data directly avoids the error entirely

Practical rule: never accept the vendor’s default segmentation without looking at it overlaid on the image, and treat any surprising “hybrid” cell type as a segmentation artifact until proven otherwise.

7.4 Cell type annotation

Aspect scRNA-seq Spatial
Starting point A real single cell with whole-transcriptome coverage. Marker-based or reference-based annotation applies directly Depends on family. Spot-based: the unit is a mixture, so the question shifts from “which type is this?” to “which types compose this, in what proportion?”. Imaging-based: the unit is a reconstructed cell, and annotation is only as trustworthy as its boundary
Method Marker scoring, or label transfer from a reference (SingleR, Azimuth, scANVI, CellTypist) Spot-based: deconvolution against a matched scRNA-seq reference (RCTD, Cell2location, SPOTlight). Imaging-based: same label-transfer logic as scRNA-seq, restricted to the panel genes
Reference dependency Helpful, not mandatory For spot-based, effectively mandatory — and the reference must match the tissue, condition and species. A mismatched reference will confidently assign cell types that are not there
Gene coverage ~20,000 genes; any marker is available Imaging platforms use a closed panel of a few hundred to a few thousand genes. If the markers distinguishing two subtypes were not designed into the panel, those subtypes are unresolvable, permanently. Panel design is therefore an annotation decision made before the experiment runs
Achievable granularity Fine-grained states (e.g. Tem vs Trm vs exhausted CD8) Spot-based: broad classes only; rare types get diluted below detection. Imaging-based: close to scRNA-seq granularity if the panel and segmentation cooperate — in practice, coarse labels are far more defensible than fine states
Output One label per cell Spot-based: a proportion vector per spot. Imaging-based: one label per segmented cell, plus a large fraction of low-count cells that resist confident labelling
Validation Marker expression, known biology, reference agreement All of the above plus spatial coherence, which is a genuinely independent check: annotated epithelium should line the lumen, germinal-centre B cells should form follicles, endothelium should trace vessels. Labels that are anatomically scattered are usually wrong
Additional analyses unlocked None — position is destroyed at dissociation Spatial domains, cellular niches and neighbourhoods, distance-to-structure analyses, and spatially constrained ligand-receptor inference

Bottom line: scRNA-seq annotation is essentially “which reference, which markers”. Spatial annotation carries a prior question that must be answered first — “do I even trust my unit of measurement?” For spot-based data that means asking how much cell-type mixing sits inside each spot; for imaging-based data it means asking how good the segmentation is. Skipping that question does not remove the error, it just makes it invisible.

One workflow, two ecosystems

R and Python provide complementary ecosystems for single-cell and spatial transcriptomics. Although package names and data structures differ, the underlying analytical workflow is largely the same.

R ecosystem Python ecosystem
Seurat and Bioconductor Scanpy and scverse
Seurat or SingleCellExperiment objects AnnData objects
Extensive statistical and visualization packages Scalable workflows and machine-learning integration

The choice of language should be guided by the biological question, the required methods, dataset size, available computing resources, and the experience of the research team.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

A typical single-cell RNA-seq workflow transforms a gene-by-cell count matrix into biologically interpretable cell populations:

Data import → Quality control → Normalization → Highly variable gene selection → Dimensionality reduction → Clustering → Cell-type annotation

1. Data import

Single-cell analysis usually starts from a gene-by-cell matrix containing raw or filtered counts. Common inputs include Cell Ranger output directories, Matrix Market files (.mtx), HDF5 files (.h5), and processed Seurat, SingleCellExperiment, or AnnData objects.

Task R Python
Read a 10x directory Seurat::Read10X() scanpy.read_10x_mtx()
Read a 10x HDF5 file Seurat::Read10X_h5() scanpy.read_10x_h5()
Main data structure Seurat / SingleCellExperiment AnnData
Common exchange formats .h5Seurat, .h5ad .h5ad, .zarr

R example


library(Seurat)

counts <- Read10X("filtered_feature_bc_matrix/")
seu <- CreateSeuratObject(counts = counts)

Python example


import scanpy as sc

adata = sc.read_10x_mtx(
    "filtered_feature_bc_matrix/",
    var_names="gene_symbols"
)

2. Quality control

Quality control aims to remove empty droplets, damaged or dying cells, multiplets, and other technical artifacts before downstream analysis.

Frequently inspected cell-level metrics include:

  • Total counts per cell.
  • Number of detected genes per cell.
  • Percentage of mitochondrial reads.
  • Additional tissue-specific metrics, such as ribosomal or hemoglobin gene expression.

Thresholds should be adapted to the tissue, protocol, sequencing depth, and expected cell populations. Fixed universal cutoffs may remove biologically relevant cells or retain technical artifacts.

Analytical task R Python
Calculate and visualize QC metrics Seurat, scater Scanpy
Identify empty droplets DropletUtils::emptyDrops() CellBender
Estimate ambient RNA contamination SoupX, DecontX CellBender
Detect doublets DoubletFinder, scDblFinder Scrublet, SOLO
Important

A high mitochondrial percentage or an unusually large number of detected genes is a warning signal, not an automatic reason to discard a cell. QC distributions should be evaluated for each dataset—and often for each sample.

3. Normalization

Cells differ in sequencing depth, library size, and RNA capture efficiency. Normalization reduces these technical differences while preserving meaningful biological variation.

Strategy R Python
Library-size normalization and log transformation Seurat::NormalizeData() scanpy.pp.normalize_total() followed by scanpy.pp.log1p()
Variance-stabilizing model Seurat::SCTransform() Pearson-residual-based methods
Deep generative model — scvi-tools

R example


seu <- NormalizeData(seu)

Python example


sc.pp.normalize_total(adata, target_sum=1e4)
sc.pp.log1p(adata)

The appropriate strategy depends on the downstream analysis. Raw counts should be preserved because some statistical methods require them.

4. Highly variable gene selection

Not every detected gene is informative for distinguishing cellular states. Highly variable genes show greater cell-to-cell variation than expected from their average expression and are commonly used for dimensionality reduction and clustering.

R Python
Seurat::FindVariableFeatures() scanpy.pp.highly_variable_genes()
scran::modelGeneVar() Scanpy implementations of commonly used selection methods

R example


seu <- FindVariableFeatures(
    seu,
    selection.method = "vst",
    nfeatures = 2000
)

Python example


sc.pp.highly_variable_genes(
    adata,
    n_top_genes=2000
)

Highly expressed genes are not necessarily highly variable genes. Feature selection focuses the analysis on genes that best describe differences among cells.

5. Dimensionality reduction

Single-cell datasets contain thousands of gene-expression dimensions. Dimensionality-reduction methods summarize this information using a smaller number of components.

Method Main purpose R Python
PCA Summarize major sources of expression variation Seurat::RunPCA() scanpy.tl.pca()
UMAP Visualize local and global cell relationships Seurat::RunUMAP() scanpy.tl.umap()
t-SNE Visualize local cell relationships Seurat::RunTSNE() scanpy.tl.tsne()

PCA is commonly used to construct the neighborhood graph and perform clustering. UMAP and t-SNE are primarily visualization methods.

Warning

UMAP coordinates are visual representations, not direct biological measurements. Cluster size, shape, separation, and distance should not be overinterpreted without additional evidence.

6. Graph construction and clustering

Cells are connected to transcriptionally similar neighbors to construct a graph. Community-detection algorithms then divide this graph into clusters.

Task R Python
Construct the neighbor graph Seurat::FindNeighbors() scanpy.pp.neighbors()
Leiden clustering Seurat::FindClusters() scanpy.tl.leiden()
Identify cluster markers Seurat::FindAllMarkers() scanpy.tl.rank_genes_groups()

The number and composition of clusters depend on choices such as the number of principal components, neighborhood size, integration strategy, and clustering resolution.

Important

A cluster is a computational result—not automatically a cell type. Clusters must be interpreted and biologically validated.

7. Cell-type annotation

Cell-type annotation assigns biological identities to computationally defined cells or clusters. Strong annotations combine multiple sources of evidence.

Marker-based annotation

  • Examine canonical and differentially expressed markers.
  • Combine positive and negative marker evidence.
  • Inspect marker co-expression rather than relying on a single gene.
  • Consider tissue, disease, developmental stage, and experimental context.

Reference-based annotation

R Python
SingleR CellTypist
Azimuth scArches
Seurat label transfer scvi-tools

Automated annotations depend on the quality and biological relevance of the reference. Predicted labels should therefore be treated as hypotheses and validated using marker expression and domain knowledge.

Take-home message

R and Python provide different but complementary implementations of the same analytical workflow. A robust analysis depends more on informed methodological choices, transparent reporting, and biological validation than on the programming language itself.
